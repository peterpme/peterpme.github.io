AI is the closest thing we've ever had to cheap, abundant intelligence. Anyone with a laptop can ask a question and get an answer that used to take an expert, a library and a week.

That's exactly why everyone wants to control it.

The debate usually gets framed as safety versus speed. I think that's the wrong frame. The real question is who gets to hand intelligence out. My answer is that it shouldn't be the government.

Here's how it goes. It starts reasonably enough: a license to train large models, a list of approved uses, a registry of who's allowed to deploy what. Each rule sounds sensible on its own. Stack them up and you get something nobody voted for directly: intelligence that's scarce on purpose.

Abundance is the whole point of AI. Control turns it back into a ration.

Once access is rationed, someone has to decide who gets what, and that's where it gets ugly. Today, if you build something useful with AI, you win. You don't need permission, just a good idea and the willingness to grind. Under state control, the question stops being "is this good?" and becomes "is this approved?"

Merit stops mattering. Compliance starts mattering.

AI becomes a sorting hat. You show up and it decides what you get: which model, how capable, for which purposes. And the state decides what the hat decides. Your access to intelligence depends less on what you can do and more on where you stand with whoever's in charge.

I don't think that's a crazy slippery slope. I think it's the default outcome of putting one entity in charge of a resource everyone needs.

What worries me most isn't censorship or surveillance, though those come along for free. It's the breadline.

When intelligence is allocated centrally, people stop building and start waiting. They wait for the approved model, then the next tier of access, then whatever version the system decides they're allowed to have. Ambition turns into paperwork and builders turn into applicants.

That's the part that rhymes with communism to me. I don't mean the flags or the slogans. I mean the mechanism: central allocation of the most valuable resource in the economy, with everyone else waiting for their share.

We know how that story goes. The people closest to the allocator eat first. Everyone else gets handouts.

This isn't theory. Look at communist Poland. After the war the country ended up [under Soviet domination](https://ipn.gov.pl/pl/historia-z-ipn/217274,11-kwietnia-1990-roku-zniesiono-Glowny-Urzad-Kontroli-Publikacji-i-Widowisk-inst.html), and the state controlled what people could read, say, and do. A [censorship office](https://ipn.gov.pl/pl/historia-z-ipn/217274,11-kwietnia-1990-roku-zniesiono-Glowny-Urzad-Kontroli-Publikacji-i-Widowisk-inst.html) checked books, newspapers, films, plays, even ads before anyone saw them. Your [passport wasn't yours](https://ipn.gov.pl/pl/historia-z-ipn/archiwum/236095,Kinga-Mierzejewska-Pawel-Pietraszek-Polski-mam-paszport-na-sercu-Akta-paszportow.html). It was kept by the police, you applied for every trip, and they could say no without a reason. When [martial law](https://eng.ipn.gov.pl/en/news/221,The-IPN-on-the-25th-anniversary-of-introducing-of-the-Martial-Law-in-Poland.html) came on December 13, 1981, more than 3,000 people were detained the first night, and about 1,200 journalists lost their jobs.

The secret police were the enforcement. They kept [files on people and their informants](https://inwentarz.ipn.gov.pl/en/glossary/d). In 1984, three Security Service officers [kidnapped Father Jerzy Popiełuszko](https://muzea.waw.pl/en/museums/popieluszko-museum/), a priest who stood with Solidarity, beat him, and threw him into a reservoir. That's what concentrated power looks like when nobody can check it. No Western government today is that. But now imagine a government with full control deciding what intelligence you're allowed to have.

To be clear, none of this requires bad people. That's what makes it dangerous. A regulator's job is to avoid blame, and the safest move is always to say no, or not yet, or fill out another form. Large incumbents quietly love strict rules, because they can afford the compliance teams and their smaller competitors can't. And every "temporary" control has a constituency that wants it to stay.

Good intentions plus bad incentives still gets you a breadline. It just takes longer.

The best argument on the other side goes something like this: AI is powerful and potentially dangerous, a handful of labs already control most of the frontier, and you can't leave something this important to a few companies and the market. Someone has to be in charge.

I take that seriously. Concentration of power in a few labs is a real problem. I don't want three companies deciding what everyone else is allowed to know either.

But look at the proposed fix. If the problem is that power over AI is too concentrated, the answer can't be to hand that power to an even bigger, even more permanent entity. That's not solving concentration, it's finishing it.

A company that misuses its power can be outcompeted. A government that does the same can't be, at least not easily, and not without a fight. You can switch models tomorrow. You can't switch governments tomorrow.

The fix for concentrated power is competition, not a bigger owner.

The other problem with "license the frontier labs" is that it hands the government a short list of companies to squeeze.

You don't need to nationalize OpenAI or Anthropic to control them. You just need to control their license. Once a handful of companies are the only ones allowed to build, the state doesn't have to own the model to decide what it says.

That's why open weights matter. A model running on your own hardware doesn't take orders from anyone's regulator.

And the squeezing doesn't have to happen in public. Any government, anywhere, can lean on a company quietly, and some already have the legal tools to do it in secret. [China's National Intelligence Law](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/national-intelligence-law-of-the-p-r-c-2017/) says all organizations "shall support, assist, and cooperate with national intelligence efforts" and "shall protect national intelligence work secrets they are aware of." In the US, an FBI [National Security Letter](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2709) can make a phone or internet company hand over customer records, and can bar it from telling anyone the FBI asked.

I'm not saying that's happening to any AI lab. I'm saying the tools exist, and a short list of licensed labs makes them a lot easier to use.

So what do I want instead? Not zero rules. I'm not arguing for chaos. Here's what I'd push for:

**Open models.** Let people run, inspect and build on AI without asking permission. Open weights are the best guarantee that no single company or government becomes the gatekeeper.

**Real competition.** More labs, more models, more choices. When one provider gets something wrong, people should be able to leave.

**Narrow rules for real harms.** Fraud is already illegal. So is abuse, and so is impersonating someone to steal from them. Enforce those laws, and write new ones where AI creates genuinely new harms. Aim the rules at what people do with the tool, not at who's allowed to own it.

That's the line I'd draw. Punish harm, don't ration capability.

When AI is cheap, open and competitive, the person who works hardest and thinks most clearly wins, no matter who they know. When it's controlled, the person closest to the controller wins.

I know which world I'd rather build in, and it's the one where intelligence is something you use, not something you're issued.

Don't let the government become the sorting hat.
